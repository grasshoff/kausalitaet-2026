Abstract

A regularity theory of causation is tested by whether its definitions recover — and, where the intuition is confused by co-occurrence, correct — the pre-theoretic verdicts of a fixed case repertoire. This volume is that repertoire. It is organised so that each fully worked case exhibits which condition of the theory does the work: minimal sufficiency against idle conjuncts, minimal necessity against the Manchester hooters, bundle-level homogeneity against naive correlation, Gate B against a confounded risk difference. Twenty-four philosophical Sprachspiele, the qualitative coincidence analyses of Volume I, and the experimental inferences of Volume II are one casebook, not three. Stub summaries are refused: a case is either carried through the inference panel or recorded in the master table with its deciding rule.

The role of intuition in causal theory

TipThe claim of this volume

A theory of causation earns its keep by recovering the stable verdicts of a fixed case repertoire from its definitions, and by correcting the unstable ones. Intuition is the test, not the analysans.

Causal intuitions as Sprachspiele

Theories of causation are tested against short case descriptions. The method is uniform across the literature: a scenario is told in a few sentences; a verdict about the relevance of one factor to another is elicited; the theory under examination is required to replicate that verdict from its definitions alone.

The theory that meets this test speaks of a few things only, and a reader who has not come from Volume I needs them before the first definition:

Key. A factor is a kind of condition or event that is either present (value 1) or absent (value 0) in a case — “the match is struck”, “the Londoners go home”. A literal is a factor with its sign: present (\(A\)) or absent (\(\neg A\)). A cluster is a combination of literals that is enough for the outcome and contains nothing idle (in the formulae: a minimally sufficient condition, MSC). A minimal theory of an outcome lists the clusters that are needed for it, none of them spare. The factor set \(\mathcal{F}\) is the explicit list of factors a case records; the causal field is the range of cases a claim is about. Minimal sufficiency is the test that no conjunct of a cluster can be dropped; minimal necessity the test that no cluster can be dropped from the theory; homogeneity the requirement that no unrecorded rival cluster acts differently across the compared cases; instantiation asks which cluster actually fired in a particular case.

In formulae \(*\) reads and, \(+\) reads or, \(\neg\) reads not. \(X \rightarrow E\) reads “\(X\) is sufficient for \(E\)”; \(X \leftrightarrow E\) “\(X\) and \(E\) are present in exactly the same cases”; \(X \Rightarrow E\) “\(X\) is a minimal theory of \(E\), written in the causal direction”. Lower-case letters (\(a\), \(e\)) name particular occurrences — the tokens of the factor types \(A\), \(E\). The definitions themselves are those of Volume I.

ImportantDefinition — causal Sprachspiel

A causal Sprachspiel is a short case description in plain language for which competent speakers converge on a causal verdict before consulting any theory. The theory must then derive that verdict from its own conditions — minimal sufficiency, minimal necessity, homogeneity, instantiation — or state explicitly why it dissents.

Stated exactly. A causal Sprachspiel is a triple \((D, V, \mathcal{F})\) where \(D\) is a case description, \(V\) is the inter-subjective verdict (“A caused E” / “A did not cause E”), and \(\mathcal{F}\) is the factor set the description makes available. The regularity theory is tested by whether it recovers \(V\) from \((D, \mathcal{F})\) using only the named conditions.

Example. The Manchester hooters (Mackie 1974, 81–84): speakers judge that the factory hooters do not cause the Londoners to go home; Mackie’s INUS analysis (a cause is an insufficient but non-redundant part of an unnecessary but sufficient condition) certifies them as a cause; minimal necessity prunes them (Volume I, section “The Manchester hooters: what minimal necessity does”; this volume, Section 2.5). The theory earns the verdict by recovering what plain-language use already secured.

The term is used here in Wittgenstein’s sense: “the meaning of a word is its use in the language” (PU §43), and grammar “tells what kind of object anything is” (PU §373). The casebook records use, not a hidden essence of cause.

This volume assembles the repertoire of the modern regularity-theoretic tradition: Graßhoff and May (2001), Baumgartner (2008, 2013), Baumgartner and Falk (2023), and Andreas and Günther (2019, 2024a, 2024b). Each fully worked case records three items:

  1. Situation — the description, quoted from the source where possible.
  2. Causal intuition — the pre-theoretic verdict, as reported in the source.
  3. Regularity judgement — the verdict the theory derives, with the condition that secures it — or an account of dissent.

A factor \(A\) is causally relevant to \(E\) if and only if \(A\) (or its negation) is a non-redundant conjunct of a cluster that is itself a non-redundant disjunct of a minimally necessary condition of \(E\) — a minimal theory of \(E\) (Graßhoff and May 2001) — and this membership is stable under suitable expansions of the factor set (Baumgartner 2013; Baumgartner and Falk 2023). The casebook shows, case by case, which of these conditions does the work.

Replication, correction, explication

Formal regularity theory stands to intuition in three relations, and only three:

  • Replication. Where the intuition is stable, the theory must recover it from the coincidence table, without unobserved primitives.
  • Correction. Where co-occurrence misleads (the hooters; Simpson’s reversal), the theory exposes the structural flaw — failure of minimal necessity (the hooters), or of homogeneity (Gate B, Simpson’s reversal).
  • Explication. Pre-theoretic language conflates type-level with token-level causation, sufficiency with necessity, risk with protective polarity. The theory distinguishes them.

The inference panel

Every fully worked case in Parts 3 and 4 follows the six-step inference procedure of Volume II (causal hypothesis inference):

  1. state the causal hypothesis;
  2. secure Gate B;
  3. run Gate A;
  4. estimate the unique contribution of the treatment’s cluster;
  5. recover and score candidate clusters;
  6. state the licensed conclusion.

The panels set these steps out under the headings query, causal field and factor set, data setup, gates, recovered hypothesis with its scores, and what would change the verdict.

Key. A contradictory configuration is a combination of recorded factor values that occurs with the outcome present and with it absent. \(C_E\) is the number of such configurations for outcome \(E\); \(B_E\), the contradiction burden, is the smallest share of rows that would have to be removed or reclassified to leave none. Gate A asks whether a table is clean enough to read a complete theory from it: no contradictory configuration, \(C_E = 0\). Gate B asks whether a two-arm comparison isolates the treatment factor: no rival cluster fires differentially with it (secured by randomisation, blocking, or an explicit bound). \(\hat{\Delta} = p_1 - p_0\) is the risk difference between the outcome rates with and without the treatment; con (consistency) is the share of cases with the cluster that have the outcome, cov (coverage) the share of outcome cases that have the cluster. INF-01–11 are the inference warrants and FALL-01–10 the fallacy inhibitors of the standard registry (Volume I).

The philosophical cases (Section 2 to Section 6) use the staged panel of models.qmd: normalisation, coincidence table, derivation, verdict — and a CVL graph, the visual language specified in causal-visuals.qmd.

The paradigm: Manchester Factory Hooters

Situation

The case owes its canonical role to Mackie (1974, 81–84); Andreas and Günther (2026, sec. 1.3) describe it as adapted from Russell (1921). Every workday at five o’clock the hooters of a Manchester factory sound; a moment later, workers in London stop work and go home. The regular succession is not accidental: knocking-off time at five o’clock sounds the hooters in Manchester and, through the automatic devices that set off the London hooters at five o’clock, sends the Londoners home.

Key. \(T\) = knocking-off time (five o’clock) · \(M\) = the Manchester factory hooters sound · \(L\) = the London workers go home · \(D\) = the Manchester hooters are sounded off-schedule (a drill or a fault) · \(B\) = an alternative cause of the Londoners’ going home (the foreman’s early dismissal). \(\neg D\) means the hooters were not sounded off-schedule. The London hooters, and the devices that set them off at five o’clock, are not a separate factor: they belong to the London-side standing conditions of the causal field and are held fixed.

Mackie himself points out that his INUS analysis counts the sounding of the Manchester hooters as a cause of the Londoners’ going home (Mackie 1974, 84; Andreas and Günther 2026, sec. 1.3); Graßhoff and May call the case “a counter example formulated by Mackie himself” (2001, 85). It shows that the INUS analysis certifies as a cause what is not one.

Causal intuition

The sounding of the Manchester hooters is not causally relevant to the London workers’ going home. The two events are parallel effects of a common cause. Mackie’s own diagnosis appeals to causal priority: preventing the Manchester hooters from sounding does not prevent the Londoners from going home, whereas preventing the London hooters does (Mackie 1974, 85–86; Andreas and Günther 2026, sec. 1.3). The regularity theory does not use this prevention test. In the reconstruction below the London hooters belong to the causal field, not to the factor set, and the verdict is earned by minimal necessity alone.

Mackie’s problem: the INUS analysis certifies the hooters

The INUS idea is that a cause is an insufficient but non-redundant part of an unnecessary but sufficient condition (INUS) (Mackie 1965). Consider the conjunction of three conditions: (i) the Manchester hooters sound; (ii) nothing made them sound off-schedule; (iii) the London-side standing conditions, including the devices that set off the London hooters at five o’clock, are in place. This conjunction is sufficient for the Londoners’ going home, and no part of it can be dropped without losing sufficiency: it is a cluster, and each of its parts is non-redundant. Thus the sounding of the Manchester hooters passes Mackie’s test for being a cause of the Londoners’ going home (Mackie 1974, 84).

Andreas and Günther (2026, sec. 1.3) state the structure as two regularities. Written with the letters of the key — their \(E_1\), \(E_2\), \(C_1\) are \(M\), \(L\), \(T\), and their auxiliary conditions \(C_2\) (Manchester side) and \(C_3\) (London side) are held fixed in the causal field — they read

\[T + D \leftrightarrow M, \qquad T + B \leftrightarrow L.\]

Reading. The Manchester hooters sound exactly when it is knocking-off time or they are sounded off-schedule; the Londoners go home exactly when it is knocking-off time or the foreman dismisses them early (diagram: Figure 3).

Then the cluster \(M * \neg D\) is minimally sufficient for \(L\): whenever the hooters sound and were not sounded off-schedule, it is five o’clock, and the Londoners go home; neither conjunct suffices alone. Each conjunct — including \(M\) — is an INUS condition of \(L\). Mackie concluded that a pure regularity theory cannot be the whole story about causation and supplemented it by a notion of causal priority (Mackie 1974, 85–86; Graßhoff and May 2001, 102–3); Baumgartner describes this as abandoning the attempt at a genuine regularity-theoretic analysis of causation (Baumgartner 2008, sec. 7).

The Graßhoff–May solution: minimal necessity

The solution within the regularity framework is due to Graßhoff and May (2001, 101–4) and May (1999): both call for the minimisation of necessary conditions (Baumgartner 2008, sec. 7). Baumgartner and Graßhoff (2004, 99–103) discuss the case in full (Baumgartner 2008, n. 21).

Mackie minimised only half of his analysis. His test removes idle conjuncts from sufficient conditions, but imposes no test on the disjunction of sufficient conditions (the list of alternative causes). The repair is the missing half: a necessary condition must be minimally necessary — no disjunct may be deletable without loss of necessity.

Applied to the hooters: the list of putative alternative causes reads

\[(M * \neg D) + T + B \leftrightarrow L.\]

Reading. The Londoners go home exactly when (the hooters sound and were not sounded off-schedule) or it is knocking-off time or the foreman dismisses them early.

Delete the hooters cluster and test what remains: \(T + B\) is still necessary for \(L\). Whenever the hooters cluster \(M * \neg D\) is instantiated, the common cause \(T\) is instantiated along with it. The hooters cluster is a free rider, and minimal necessity removes it. Since \(M\) appears nowhere in the minimal theory of \(L\), the Manchester hooters are not causally relevant to the Londoners’ going home.

What the elimination leaves behind is the structure the intuition had all along:

The general lesson is worth stating separately, because the pattern recurs throughout the casebook. Wherever two factors correlate perfectly and one is tempted to call the earlier a cause of the later, minimal necessity asks whether the cluster through the earlier one is needed once the common cause is in the theory. If it is not, it goes — and the correlation is explained without a causal link between the correlates.

Token-level actual causation

Type-level membership does not settle which cluster fired in a particular situation. Actual causation asks which instantiated cluster brought about this outcome.

Case 7: Two fires (symmetric overdetermination)

Situation. Two fires, ignited independently by short circuit (\(A\)) and lightning (\(B\)), reach a building at the same moment and burn it down (\(E\)). The structure is Baumgartner’s symmetric overdetermination, where two neurons “trigger E simultaneously, such that each stimulus would have itself been sufficient for E to fire” (Baumgartner 2013, fig. 4c); the fire story is an illustration of it.

Intuition. Both \(a\) and \(b\) — the particular short circuit and the particular lightning strike — are actual causes of \(e\), the burning of this building.

Regularity judgement. Both clusters fire in this case. Actual causation certifies both clusters as active. The case shows that minimality at type level does not force uniqueness at token level: overdetermination is membership of two instantiated clusters, not a defect of the theory.

Case 8: Suzie and Billy (late preemption)

Situation. Suzie (\(A\)) and Billy (\(B\)) both throw rocks at a bottle; the bottle shatters (\(E\)). Suzie’s rock arrives first and shatters it; Billy’s rock passes through empty air. Billy’s process is cut off by the effect itself — the pattern Baumgartner analyses as late preemption (Baumgartner 2013, fig. 4b); the rock story is an illustration of it.

Intuition. Suzie’s throw caused the shattering; Billy’s did not.

Regularity judgement. Suzie’s throw belongs to the active cluster. Billy’s cluster is preempted: it is not completely instantiated, because his rock never meets an intact bottle. Actual causation tracks the instantiated cluster, not the counterfactual “had Suzie not thrown, Billy would have.”

The remaining token-level cases (rows 9–16 of Table 1) vary preemption, switching, prevention, and omission. Each is decided by the rule named in the table.

Explanatory ‘because’

Andreas and Günther (2019, 2024a, 2024b) extend regularity theory to explanatory ‘because’ claims. The load-bearing contrast is the tower and its shadow: the tower explains the shadow, the shadow does not explain the tower. The remaining explanatory cases (rows 17–24 of Table 1) — among them the subcause, extended double prevention and the assassin’s mentor — are recorded there with their deciding rules. The Munich–Lund example, with which Andreas and Günther show that the unstrengthened Ramsey test makes ‘because’ trivial (2019, secs. 1, 3.3), is not a row of the table.

FH alone is refuted by rows 3, 5 and 6. Two minimally sufficient conditions contain an FH literal, \(\text{SH}*\neg\text{FH}\) and \(\text{AL}*\neg\text{FH}\), each witnessed only by row 4. Both are free riders: \(\text{AL}*\text{SH}\) alone is already necessary for WU, so (MN) removes them.

Causal hypothesis. \(\text{AL}*\text{SH} \Rightarrow \text{WU}\).

Reading. (AL and SH) are causally relevant to WU. The theory has a single cluster, so its direction comes from the process order AL \(\prec\) WU, rule (CPLX)(b); without a process order the engine reports such a theory with the status UNORIENTABLE. The factory hooters occur in no cluster: they and the waking are both preceded by the scheduled shift, and the table does not show the shift to be a cause of the hooters (row 4 has a scheduled shift without hooters, row 5 hooters without a shift).

Licensed verdict — NO. FH is not causally relevant to WU.

Warrants. INF-11 (no cluster with an FH literal survives minimal necessity). The common-cause warrant INF-08 is not licensed by these six rows.

Case B-ST: Statin Trial — Does STATIN Reduce Cardiovascular Events?

NoteCase B-ST — Does STATIN reduce cardiovascular events in HBP patients?

Query. Is STATIN causally relevant to \(\neg\)CVE (event-free survival) in the causal field of the trial population — 400 patients, half of them with a history of high blood pressure?

Causal field and factor set. \(\mathcal{F} = \{\text{STATIN}, \text{HBP}, \text{CVE}\}\) — statin therapy, history of high blood pressure, cardiovascular event. Process order: HBP \(\prec\) STATIN \(\prec\) CVE — the history of high blood pressure precedes the therapy, which precedes the event. Polarity: outcome is CVE; causal hypothesis is for the protective direction (\(\neg\)CVE).

Coincidence data. 400 patients, 200 per arm. Stratified counts:

HBP \(=1\) HBP \(=0\)
STATIN \(=1\) CVE \(5/100\) CVE \(19/100\)
STATIN \(=0\) CVE \(30/100\) CVE \(18/100\)

Aggregate rates: \(p_1 = 24/200 = 0.12\) (STATIN), \(p_0 = 48/200 = 0.24\) (control). Each arm holds 100 patients with and 100 without a history of high blood pressure.

Gate B. Randomisation performed; no baseline imbalance in known risk factors. Gate B is satisfied: the difference-test is valid.

Gate A. Within \(\mathcal{F} = \{\text{STATIN}, \text{HBP}, \text{CVE}\}\) each of the four configurations of STATIN and HBP occurs with and without CVE: \(C_E = 4\), \(B_E = 72/400 = 0.18\). Gate A is violated: this factor set is incomplete for sufficiency-reading. That is expected in difference-tests and does not invalidate Gate B.

Risk difference. \(\hat{\Delta} = p_1 - p_0 = -0.12\) (protective). Fisher exact \(p = 0.003\) (two-sided, \(0.0026\)).

MSC search. The difference-test is carried by the HBP\(=1\) stratum (\(5\%\) vs \(30\%\)); in the HBP\(=0\) stratum the rates are \(19\%\) and \(18\%\). HBP is therefore the conjunctive partner of STATIN. The engine recovers:

\[\text{STATIN} * \text{HBP} \Rightarrow \neg\text{CVE} \qquad [\text{con} = 95/100 = 0.95,\ \text{cov} = 95/328 \approx 0.29].\]

Reading. Statin therapy and a history of high blood pressure are causally relevant to the absence of a cardiovascular event (diagram: Volume II, the statin figure of the introduction). The coverage of \(0.29\) records that most of the 328 event-free patients are covered by other clusters for \(\neg\)CVE, which this factor set does not name: the absence of an event is equifinal.

Polarity. STATIN is a literal in an MSC for \(\neg\text{CVE}\) (protective).

Licensed verdict — YES (protective polarity). Under Gate B, \(\hat{\Delta} < 0\) licenses STATIN as a literal of an MSC for \(\neg\)CVE, not for CVE: STATIN is a non-redundant literal of the cluster \(\text{STATIN}*\text{HBP}\) for the absence of a cardiovascular event.

Warrants. INF-01, INF-04. INF-02 requires consistency \(1.0\) and is not met (\(\text{con} = 0.95\)).

Case B-16: Simpson’s Paradox — Kidney Stone Treatments

NoteCase B-16 — Is open surgery (T) causally relevant to treatment success (E) in kidney stone patients?

Query. Is T (open surgery, against percutaneous nephrolithotomy) causally relevant to E (treatment success)? Data: Charig et al. (1986), 700 patients.

Causal field and factor set. \(\mathcal{F} = \{\text{T}, \text{SEV}, \text{E}\}\) where SEV = stone severity (large vs. small).

Data setup (stratified 2×2 table).

Small stones Large stones Total
Open surgery 81/87 = 93.1% 192/263 = 73.0% 273/350 = 78.0%
Percutaneous 234/270 = 86.7% 55/80 = 68.8% 289/350 = 82.6%

Open surgery does better in both strata — by \(6.4\) percentage points for small stones and \(4.3\) for large ones — but worse overall (\(-4.6\) points), because large stones were assigned to it about \(3.3\) times as often: \(263\) of its \(350\) patients had large stones, against \(80\) of the \(350\) percutaneous patients.

Gate B. Non-randomised observational study. SEV co-varies with T assignment — Gate B is violated in the aggregate analysis.

Gate A. Over \(\mathcal{F} = \{\text{T}, \text{SEV}, \text{E}\}\): all four configurations of T and SEV carry both outcomes, so \(C_E = 4\), and \(B_E = (6 + 71 + 36 + 25)/700 = 138/700 = 0.197\). Homogeneity falsified.

Licensed verdict — SUSPENDED. The framework declines to issue a causal verdict on the aggregate data.

FALL codes. FALL-04 (Simpson’s paradox, SEVERE): the aggregate risk difference (\(-4.6\) points) has the opposite sign to both strata (\(+6.4\) and \(+4.3\)).

Diagnosis. The reversal is compositional confounding. Stone size lowers the success rate under both treatments, and it travels with assignment: large stones went mostly to open surgery. That violates Gate B in the aggregate. It does not show SEV to be a conjunctive partner of T: both strata favour open surgery by similar margins (the two margins differ by \(2.2\) points), whereas a conjunction \(\text{T}*\text{SEV}\) would show the treatment making its difference in one stratum and not in the other, as HBP does for STATIN in Case B-ST. Within the strata the comparison is closer to Gate B, but in a non-randomised study nothing secures that no further factor travelled with assignment; the verdict therefore stays suspended.

Case B-18: Underpowered Study — Non-Significance vs. Absence

NoteCase B-18 — Does non-significance establish causal irrelevance?

Query. A trial of \(n = 50\) per arm reports 15 events on control against 19 on treatment, \(\hat{\Delta} = 0.08\), Fisher exact \(p = 0.53\). Does this establish that T is not causally relevant to E?

Causal field and factor set. \(\mathcal{F} = \{\text{T}, \text{E}\}\). Outcome E: adverse event.

Data setup. \(n = 50\) per arm. Control rate 30%, treatment rate 38%.

Gate B. Randomisation performed; Gate B secured by design.

Power analysis. Achieved power is 0.13. Detecting a difference of \(0.08\) from a control rate of \(0.30\) at 80% power and two-sided \(\alpha = 0.05\) requires \(n = 550\) per arm by the two-proportion formula of Volume II (section “Power from the identity”). The study is severely underpowered.

Licensed verdict — UNCERTAIN / INCONCLUSIVE. Non-significance in an underpowered study does not license a claim of absence.

FALL codes. FALL-07 (underpowered absence claim); FALL-08 (absence of evidence taken as evidence of absence).

Case B-LK: LK-99 Ambient Superconductivity Audit

NoteCase B-LK — LK-99 Ambient Superconductivity Claim Audit

Query. Does the material LK-99 exhibit ambient-pressure superconductivity (zero resistance, ZERO_R \(=1\))?

Background. Sukbae Lee et al. (2023) claimed zero resistance and levitation in \(\mathrm{Pb}_{10-x}\mathrm{Cu}_x(\mathrm{PO}_4)_6\mathrm{O}\).

Factor set & Data setup. \(\mathcal{F} = \{\text{LK99}, \text{CU\_SUB}, \text{CU2S}, \text{ZERO\_R}, \text{DROP}\}\). Key. LK99 = sample of the LK-99 compound · CU_SUB = copper substituted on lead sites · CU2S = \(\mathrm{Cu}_2\mathrm{S}\) impurity present · ZERO_R = zero resistance · DROP = sharp drop in resistivity near \(377\text{ K}\).

  • Original claim: \(\text{LK99} * \text{CU\_SUB} \Rightarrow \text{ZERO\_R}\) — the compound with copper substitution is sufficient for zero resistance.
  • Replication base: pure single crystals of LK-99 without \(\mathrm{Cu}_2\mathrm{S}\), grown at the Max Planck Institute for Solid State Research, Stuttgart (Puphal et al., 2023); the identification of the \(\mathrm{Cu}_2\mathrm{S}\) phase transition as the source of the resistivity drop (Jain, University of Illinois Urbana-Champaign, 2023), and tests of samples with different \(\mathrm{Cu}_2\mathrm{S}\) content by a team at the Chinese Academy of Sciences (Zhu et al., 2023).

Gate A Analysis. Pure LK-99 single crystals (\(\text{CU2S}=0\)) are insulators. Samples with \(\mathrm{Cu}_2\mathrm{S}\) impurities show a sharp resistivity drop near \(377\text{ K}\) (\(104^\circ\text{C}\)), the temperature of the known structural phase transition of \(\mathrm{Cu}_2\mathrm{S}\). No sample shows zero resistance.

Over the claim’s own factors \(\{\text{LK99}, \text{CU\_SUB}\}\), the configuration LK99 \(=1\), CU_SUB \(=1\) occurs with the resistivity drop (impure samples) and without it (pure crystals): a contradictory configuration, \(C_E > 0\). Adding CU2S to the factor set resolves it: the drop occurs exactly when CU2S \(=1\).

Licensed verdict — FALSIFIED. The claim \(\text{LK99} * \text{CU\_SUB} \Rightarrow \text{ZERO\_R}\) is refuted by the replication record: the compound is present without zero resistance. The resistivity drop is accounted for by the \(\mathrm{Cu}_2\mathrm{S}\) phase transition, \(\text{CU2S} \Rightarrow \text{DROP}\) (in words: the \(\mathrm{Cu}_2\mathrm{S}\) impurity is causally relevant to the drop; one cluster, oriented by the process order of heating through the transition).

FALL codes. FALL-03 (confounding: contradictory configuration in the replication record), FALL-02 (naive correlation: the drop was attributed to LK99 without a sufficient condition that survives the replication record).

Case B-OP: OPERA Faster-Than-Light Neutrino Audit

NoteCase B-OP — OPERA Faster-Than-Light Neutrino Speed Audit

Query. Do muon neutrinos travel faster than light (\(v > c\))?

Background. The OPERA Collaboration (2011) reported that neutrinos travelling from CERN to Gran Sasso arrived \(60.7\text{ ns}\) earlier than light would.

Factor set & Data setup. \(\mathcal{F} = \{\text{NEUTRINO}, \text{FIBER\_LOOSE}, \text{CLOCK\_CAL}, \text{SUPERLUMINAL}\}\). Key. NEUTRINO = a CNGS muon-neutrino event · FIBER_LOOSE = the optical fibre to the master clock improperly connected · CLOCK_CAL = the master-clock oscillator off its calibration · SUPERLUMINAL = an early arrival measured.

Gate A & Diagnostic Analysis. In 2012 OPERA identified two instrumental effects of opposite sign:

  1. An improperly connected optical fibre bringing the GPS time signal to the OPERA master clock, which added \(73.2\text{ ns}\) to the timing chain and made the neutrinos appear to arrive early.
  2. A mis-calibrated 10 MHz oscillator of the master clock, running slightly above its specified frequency, which lengthened the measured time of flight.

With both corrections the published result is an early arrival of \(\delta t = (6.5 \pm 7.4\,(\text{stat.})\,^{+8.3}_{-8.0}\,(\text{sys.}))\text{ ns}\), consistent with \(v = c\).

Licensed verdict — FALSIFIED / SYSTEMATIC ARTEFACT. Neutrinos do not exceed light speed. The \(60.7\text{ ns}\) anomaly was an artefact of the timing chain, chiefly the improperly connected fibre.

FALL codes. The fallacy registry (FALL-01–10) has no inhibitor for a systematic measurement artefact; the case marks that gap.
