Causality.
Eight cases, one definition.

The Manchester hooters sound every day at five, and in London the workers go home; ice-cream sales and drownings rise together; one says a short circuit caused a fire, although short circuits are neither sufficient nor necessary for fires. The regularity theory of causal relevance after Graßhoff and May uses a single definition to distinguish the factors that are causally relevant to an effect from those that merely occur together with it regularly. This site tells it through eight cases, step by step, on diagrams and on the tables of cases.

Gerd Graßhoff, Humboldt-Universität zu Berlin, Research Institute of the Deutsches Museum

The theory goes back to Graßhoff and May (2001) and May (1999). The three volumes are working drafts (as of 29 September 2026) and are provided here for discussion.

The paradigm case

The Manchester hooters

Mackie turned the case against his own INUS analysis: it lets the Manchester hooters count as a cause of the London workers going home. In eight chapters, step by step on the table of cases, the narrative shows which half of the minimisation Mackie left out and how minimal necessity strikes out the hooters cluster. What remains is what intuition always said: two effects of one cause.

One definition

A factor is a kind of circumstance, such as "short circuit" or "oxygen present", that in each single case is either present or absent. Every study records a definite set of factors; one of them is the effect whose causally relevant factors are sought.

A cluster of an effect is an and-combination of conditions, each of which reads "this factor is present" or "this factor is absent". It meets two requirements: in every case in which all its conditions hold, the effect occurs; and if a single condition is left out, this no longer holds for the rest. A cluster is thus sufficient for the effect and contains no superfluous condition; it is therefore also called a minimally sufficient condition. Each of its conditions is indispensable to it.

A minimal theory of an effect is an or-combination (disjunction) of clusters of that effect that meets two corresponding requirements: in every case in which the effect occurs, at least one of its clusters holds; and if a single cluster is struck out, there is a case with the effect in which none of the remaining clusters holds. The or-combination is thus necessary for the effect and contains no superfluous cluster.

A factor is causally relevant to an effect if and only if, for every set of factors that contains it and the effect, there is a minimal theory of the effect formed from that set in which its presence or its absence is a condition of a cluster.

The requirement holds for every such set of factors, not only for the one a study happens to have recorded. A factor can stand in a cluster as long as another factor that occurs together with it is not recorded, and drop out of every minimal theory as soon as that factor is added; it is then not causally relevant. A finding about the recorded factors therefore supports the proposition that a factor is causally relevant; it does not prove it: a study with further factors can refute it. Causal relevance is not a degree; a factor is causally relevant to an effect or it is not.

The second minimisation, that of the or-combination, is the step Mackie's INUS analysis lacked; the hooters narrative shows what it achieves. The definition needs no counterfactual assumptions, no interventions and no probabilities. Probabilities enter only in Volume II, where experimental data are noisy.

How to read the diagrams

Each diagram renders a minimal theory sign for sign, just as its formula does. Four rules are enough.

  1. Every arrow runs from a factor to the effect and stands for a condition of a cluster. Different clusters are alternatives: each is sufficient for the effect on its own ("or", in formulas +).
  2. A golden arc ties the arrows of one cluster: only all of these conditions together are sufficient ("and", in formulas *). An arrow without an arc is a cluster of a single condition.
  3. A box with ¬ on the arrow means: the condition of the cluster is not the presence but the absence of the factor.
  4. A hollow head stands for a regularity over kinds of events. A filled head stands for a single case: it marks the cluster whose conditions were all met in that case.
