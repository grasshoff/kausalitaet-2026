Causality.
Eight cases, one definition.

The Manchester hooters sound every day at five, and in London the workers go home; ice-cream sales and drownings rise together; a short circuit causes a fire although it is neither sufficient nor necessary for it. The regularity theory of causal relevance after Graßhoff and May separates causes from mere companions with a single definition. This site tells it through eight cases, step by step, on diagrams and on the tables of cases.

Gerd Graßhoff, Humboldt-Universität zu Berlin, Research Institute of the Deutsches Museum

The theory goes back to Graßhoff and May (2001) and May (1999). The three volumes are working drafts (as of 29 September 2026) and are provided here for discussion.

The paradigm case

The Manchester hooters

Mackie turned the case against his own INUS analysis: it lets the Manchester hooters count as a cause of the London workers going home. In eight chapters, step by step on the table of cases, the narrative shows which half of the minimisation Mackie left out and how minimal necessity strikes out the hooters cluster. What remains is what intuition always said: two effects of one cause.

One definition

A factor is causally relevant to an effect if it is an indispensable part of a cluster of a minimal theory of that effect and remains so however far the set of recorded factors is extended.

A cluster is a minimally sufficient condition: factors that are sufficient together and none of which may be missing. A minimal theory is a disjunction of such clusters that is necessary for the effect and from which no cluster can be deleted without it ceasing to be necessary. The second minimisation, that of the disjunction, is the step Mackie's INUS analysis lacked; the hooters narrative shows what it achieves. The definition needs no counterfactual assumptions, no interventions and no probabilities. Volume II works with probabilities only where experimental data are noisy.

How to read the diagrams

The diagrams match the formula of a minimal theory element for element. Four rules are enough.

  1. Every arrow runs from a factor to the effect. Arrows without an arc are alternatives: each suffices on its own ("or", in formulas +).
  2. A golden arc ties the arrows of one cluster: these factors suffice only together ("and", in formulas *).
  3. A box with ¬ on the arrow means: not the factor but its absence takes part.
  4. A hollow head stands for a regularity over kinds of events, a filled head for the cluster that fired in one particular case.
